More inputs, one cut

A neuron draws one straight cut. With two inputs that's a line across a square, and four corners are easy to picture. What happens with three inputs? With n?

Short answer: the cut becomes a flat sheet, then something you can't draw, and it gets much pickier about what it can separate. Drag, click and train below.

Chapter 1

The square, in one breath

Two inputs, each 0 or 1, make four possible situations: the corners of a square. Colour each corner “fires” or “doesn't”, and that's one rule. There are 24 = 16 rules. A single line can separate the fires from the doesn'ts in 14 of them. The two it can't do are XOR (“exactly one”) and its opposite, where the two diagonals share a colour.

Now keep the same game and add a third input.

Chapter 2

Three inputs: a cube

Three inputs make eight situations, the corners of a cube. The neuron's score is still a weighted sum w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + w₃x₃ + b, and “score above 0” is still the rule. But the boundary (score exactly 0) is now a flat plane slicing through the cube. Corners on one side fire; the rest don't.

Try AND, OR and majority: each gets a plane, and you can see it clip the cube's edges. Then press Random a dozen times. Of the 28 = 256 possible rules, a plane can do 104, about 41%. (I checked: the trainer finds a cut for exactly 104 of the 256.) In 2D it was 14 of 16, nearly 88%. The share is already dropping.

Predict first: which rule would be the hardest one to cut?
Chapter 3

The one that never works: parity

Press parity (fires when an odd number of inputs are on). Every corner now has the opposite colour to all three of its neighbours: all 12 edges are red-meets-blue. A plane would have to cut every single edge. Watch the trainer flail: the best it manages is guessing.

You don't need to trust the trainer, though. Fix one input at 0 and look only at the other two: that's the square again, and parity there is XOR. A plane, cut down to a square face, is just a line. No line does XOR, so no plane does parity. The same trick works at any n: fix all but two inputs and you're back at XOR on a square.

Another one worth a click: exactly one. That's part 1's “exactly one” problem, and at 3 inputs it's still out of reach.

A neuron isn't limited by how many inputs it has. It's limited by the shape of the rule: only “a weighted sum crosses a threshold”.
Chapter 4

Now n inputs

The pattern continues: n inputs give 2n corners of a hypercube, the neuron cuts it with a flat hyperplane (a line at n=2, a sheet at n=3, and from n=4 up nothing you can draw, but the same weighted sum), and the number of possible rules explodes much faster than the number of cuttable ones.

By n=8 a neuron is a tiny sliver of all possible rules: roughly 1 in 1064. Still, almost no rule you'd ever want is a random one. “Is the number odd?” was one cut because the last digit alone decides it. The real catch is that the cuttable rules are a special family: the answer has to move in one direction as each input increases, weighted. Parity has the opposite shape, since every flip reverses it.

The counts are the known numbers of linearly separable Boolean functions (OEIS A000609). I confirmed n = 2 (14) and n = 3 (104) by brute force in the trainer above.

Chapter 5

Parity at n, and the way out

Same slider as above. Train one neuron on n-bit parity, then give the problem a hidden layer.

parity of n bits
Pick n above, then press a button.

The hidden layer idea is nicely small. Neuron k asks one plain question: “are at least k inputs on?” (a single cut, so a neuron can do it). Count s = how many inputs are on, and the first s hidden neurons fire. The output neuron adds them with alternating signs: +h₁ − h₂ + h₃ − …. That's 1 when s is odd and 0 when it's even. Parity, with n neurons in the middle and nothing learned yet: I just wrote the weights. Training can find solutions like this on its own, which is what the next experiment is for.

One neuron: one cut, fewer and fewer rules as inputs grow. A hidden layer: cuts of cuts, and the parity wall falls.
